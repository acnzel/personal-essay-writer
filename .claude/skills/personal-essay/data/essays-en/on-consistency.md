# On Consistency

On my way home from work recently, I passed by the gym and suddenly thought: that 3-month membership I signed up for at the beginning of last year—how many days did I actually go? Twenty times? No, honestly it was probably less than fifteen. Every time I told myself "really starting tomorrow," but in the end, the membership expired and I was still the same.

But strangely, the English study I started around the same time has continued for 6 months. 15 minutes a day, on the subway during my commute. It doesn't seem like much, but it's accumulated to over 90 hours. The gym and English study—what was the difference?

The answer was surprisingly simple. For the gym, I had to debate "Should I go today or not?" every time. For English, I just automatically started when I got on the subway. Consistency turned out to be not a matter of willpower but a matter of systems.

## The Importance of Routines

It's easy to think of consistency as "a characteristic of people with strong willpower." But in my experience, consistent people often have well-designed systems rather than strong willpower.

People pay a decision-making cost every time they have to form a new resolve. When questions like "Should I exercise today?", "Should I read now?", "Can I put it off?" pile up, you eventually get exhausted. Psychology calls this decision fatigue. If even important work decisions are tiring, imagine having to decide on trivial daily routines every time.

Conversely, routines automate behavior. When a trigger occurs, you execute without thinking. Like "After brushing teeth, stretching" or "Before brewing coffee, 3 pages of reading." The power of routines is making it so you don't ask "Should I or shouldn't I?"

Also, routines work like basic infrastructure that stabilizes the quality of life. Developers know this: just like any good feature will crash if the infrastructure is unstable, if foundational routines like sleep, exercise, and learning are shaky, performance becomes inconsistent too. Because consistency comes from repetition of ordinary days, not extraordinary ones.

## How to Form Routines

The biggest mistake when creating routines is "starting grandiose." I did too. I signed up for the gym and planned to work out 5 times a week, 1 hour each. The first week I powered through on willpower, but starting the second week, it collapsed.

Looking back now, routines need to start as a minimum viable unit (MVP). Not a perfect routine, but a routine that can be repeated without failure—that comes first.

**Fix the trigger**

Rather than vague conditions like "in the morning," concrete triggers attached to actions are better. Things like "after brushing teeth," "before brewing coffee," "before taking off shoes after coming home from work." Attach them to things you already do every day and you won't forget.

**Start with 2-5 minutes**

Routines aren't about powering through with willpower—they're a game of lowering resistance to build habits. Not an hour of exercise but 10 squats, not a whole book but 3 pages. It might sound ridiculous, but this is real. If you do a 2-minute routine for 30 days, your body remembers. Then you can increase it.

**Define by action, not result**

"Read one book" is a goal, but a routine should be a unit you can execute today, like "read 3 pages daily." You can't control results, but you can control actions.

**Stack routines**

Attach new routines to existing habits. I linked "brew coffee → 3 minutes stretching → start work." Each one is small, but when connected like a chain, it's hard to break.

Routines should start at a level where the current you—not the "ideal you"—can repeat without failure. One thing you can definitely do tomorrow morning is more important than your impressive image 6 months later.

## Managing Routine Sustainability

Routines are harder to operate (Ops) than to create. The first 2 weeks are novel so you do well, but starting week 3, things start to break down. Business trips, overtime, illness, appointments... variables are always there.

So for sustainability, you need "design that's recoverable when broken."

**Abandon perfectionism and set an SLO**

Rather than 100% achievement, set a service level objective like "success if 5 times per week." Then if one day fails, the system survives. The biggest reason routines break is that one day of failure leads to "it's ruined anyway, just give up." If you aim for 80-90% achievement from the start, it's okay to take a day off.

**Keep only 1 observable metric**

Checklists, calendars, habit apps—anything is fine, but records should be simple. I just mark O on Google Calendar. If recording becomes annoying, routines collapse. If monitoring is complicated, no one looks. It's the same as development operations.

**Create a failure response plan**

Not "if I missed it, double tomorrow" but prepare fallback routines like "on days I can't, the 1-minute version." If 30 minutes of exercise is burdensome, at least 5 squats. If 10 pages of reading won't work, at least 1 page. Just like keeping a service running in degraded mode when there's an outage, surviving in reduced mode for routines is important. 1% is better than zero.

**Fix review cycles**

Even briefly, like 10 minutes on Sundays or the first week of every month, checking "what triggers worked well / where did I get stuck" keeps improving routines. Without retrospectives, the same mistakes repeat. I spend just 5 minutes every Sunday evening fine-tuning next week's routines. If "stretching after starting work" isn't working, I change the trigger to "stretching right after brushing teeth."

---

Consistency is structure, not emotion. It's possible not because willpower is strong, but because the system is well-designed.

If you make routines small to fit yourself and operate them so they can be reattached even when broken, at some point consistency becomes not "effort" but "default." Like brushing teeth daily, like going to work daily, you just do it.

I'm still not perfect. There are weeks when routines break, and I repeat the process of rebuilding them. But compared to me 6 months ago, I've definitely changed. 90 hours of English study, 12 books, 100 pre-work stretches. Each one isn't impressive, but accumulated, it feels quite substantial.

Why not start just one thing? It doesn't need to be grand. 2 minutes is enough. Just repeat those 2 minutes for 30 days. At some point, you'll find your default has changed.
